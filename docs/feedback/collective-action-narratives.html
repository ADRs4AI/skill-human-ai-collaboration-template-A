Collective Action Problems in Literature

Stories about coordination failures—where no villain exists, only a system of incentives—are strikingly rare. Their scarcity reveals something profound about how human cognition works. This presentation explores what collective action problems are, why stories about them are so rare, and what the stories that do exist can teach us.

The Pattern

  • No villain, only a system of incentives
  • Rational individuals become collective fools
  • Nash equilibrium nobody chose
  • Nash equilibrium nobody can escape
The defining feature: the antagonist is not a person, but a structure. Common antagonist: "a Nash equilibrium that nobody chose but nobody can escape."

Three Defining Features

  1. Locally rational, globally disastrous
    Each individual's choice makes sense given constraints
  2. Knowledge doesn't help
    Everyone knows the system is broken but can't escape
  3. No individual villain
    The system is the antagonist
These three features distinguish coordination failures from other problems. Even knowing about the trap doesn't enable escape—that's what makes it a true coordination failure.

Tragedy of the Commons

Shared pasture, multiple herders:

  • Each herder adds cattle (rational choice)
  • Benefit: individual profit
  • Cost: overgrazing (distributed)
  • Result: pasture destroyed, everyone loses
The commons is degraded by individually rational action
Classic framing by Garrett Hardin (1968), though the concept is ancient. This is the archetypal coordination failure: rational at micro level, catastrophic at macro level.

Why These Stories Are Rare

STACK: Why Stories Are Rare ───────────────── 1. Our brains evolved to detect agents 2. Narrative grammar requires villains 3. Systems resist character identification ───────────────── NAVIGATE: ↓ to dive in · → to skip to the stories that exist

We See Agents, Not Systems

Hyperactive Agency Detection Device (HADD)

  • Evolved to over-detect intentional agents
  • We see who before what
  • Faces in clouds before climate patterns
Conspiracy theories are failed systemic narratives that revert to agent-detection
Cost of failing to detect predator (death) >> cost of false detection (wasted vigilance). Every time someone replaces "the system produces bad outcomes" with "they're doing this to us on purpose," HADD is winning.

Narrative Grammar Needs Villains

Vladimir Propp's 31 functions, 7 character types:

  • Villain, Hero, Helper, Donor
  • Princess, Dispatcher, False Hero

No structural role for:
"Pareto-suboptimal equilibrium"

Greimas's actantial model requires an opponent—an entity that acts. Writing guides explicitly recommend against non-agent antagonists: "the antagonist should be a person, not a force of nature, a group, or a general life condition."

Systems Resist Identification

  • Character identification drives narrative immersion
  • Systems have no interiority to access
  • No emotional throughline to follow
Stories about systems are systematically less engaging than stories about villains
Narrative transportation theory: we enter stories through characters. Hollywood routinely adds personified villains to systemic source material because the Hero's Journey template requires one.

Folk Narratives

The stories that do exist

Belling the Cat

Medieval fable, attributed to Aesop

Mice hold council. Young mouse proposes: tie bell around cat's neck. Everyone cheers—until old mouse asks: who will bell the cat?

Silence. Nothing is done.

  • Solution exists, everyone agrees
  • But cost falls on one, benefit shared by all
The purest folk expression of collective action problem. So politically potent it was used in Piers Plowman (1377) about Parliament's inability to check royal power, and by Scottish Lord Gray (1482) about confronting King James III. The fable persists because it diagnoses without prescribing.

The Four Oxen and the Lion

Four oxen graze together, tails facing outward. Lion cannot attack. Lion whispers slanders—makes them graze separately. Lion defeats them one by one.

The antagonist is the division, not the predator

Aesop's tale showing how coordination breakdown enables external threats. The lion is opportunistic but not the real problem—the real problem is the loss of coordination.

The Allegory of the Long Spoons

Found in Jewish, Buddhist, Hindu, Chinese, and Christian traditions

Heaven and hell have identical physical setup: tables laden with food, spoons too long for self-feeding.

Hell: everyone starves trying to feed themselves
Heaven: everyone thrives feeding each other

Same game, different equilibrium

Perhaps the cleanest coordination game in world folklore. Physical setup identical, only the equilibrium differs. Shows how coordination can make all the difference.

Stone Soup

European folk tale

Travelers arrive in village. No one will share food (free-rider problem). Travelers announce they'll make "stone soup"—just need to boil a stone. Villagers curious. Travelers say: "would be better with carrots..." Each villager contributes small ingredient. Result: feast for all.

The rare positive coordination story

Stone functions as what game theorists call a Schelling point—a focal point enabling coordination. Sequential small commitments overcome the free-rider problem.

Epistemological Coordination Failures

Collective delusion through incentives

Cassandra ───────────────── NAVIGATE: ↓ to dive in · → to skip to epic literature These are the most intellectually rich category: stories about how societies lose the ability to know what they know.

The Emperor's New Clothes

Hans Christian Andersen, 1837

Weavers claim cloth is "invisible to anyone unfit for their position." No one admits they can't see it. Emperor parades naked. Child breaks the delusion: "He has nothing on!"

Pluralistic ignorance: no one believes, but everyone believes that everyone else believes

Based on Spanish tale from 1335 where test was genealogical (invisible to illegitimate sons). Andersen shifted to meritocratic anxiety. The swindlers don't need to convince anyone the clothes are beautiful—just make cost of admitting truth higher than cost of lying. Child "solves" it only because outside incentive structure.

The Boy Who Cried Wolf

Aesop

Shepherd boy repeatedly raises false alarms for entertainment. Villagers respond at first. Eventually stop believing. When wolf actually comes, no one responds. Sheep are killed.

Tragedy of the epistemic commons

Usually read as individual moral lesson about lying. Deeper structure: community's alarm system is shared resource. Boy's exploitation depletes the collective asset of trust. When truth-telling is needed, infrastructure for believing truth has been destroyed.

The Cassandra Myth

Apollo grants Cassandra true prophecy, then curses her so no one will believe. She warns about Troy's fall, Trojan Horse, Agamemnon's murder—all ignored. Troy falls. Cassandra dies.

No solution at all

Curse operates on social system's ability to process accurate information. Even when her brother Helenus gives identical predictions and IS believed, Troy still cannot coordinate effectively. Cost of believing (acknowledging danger, changing course) exceeds cost of denying. Unlike Emperor's New Clothes, purely diagnostic—offers no resolution.

A Trilogy of Epistemic Loss

  1. Emperor's New Clothes
    How incentives prevent truth-telling
  2. Boy Who Cried Wolf
    How trust infrastructure gets depleted
  3. Cassandra
    How societies can't act on truth even when spoken
These three stories form a progression about how societies lose the ability to know what they know. From preventing speech → destroying trust → inability to act on truth.

Epic Literature

The Mahabharata's Dice Game

~400 BCE–400 CE

Yudhishthira accepts rigged dice game. Progressively loses wealth, kingdom, brothers, himself, wife Draupadi. Draupadi dragged to assembly for public humiliation. Every elder—Bhishma, Drona, Vidura—remains silent despite knowing injustice. Bhishma: "bound by dharma to the throne."

Each individual's silence reinforces the others'

Perhaps most profound literary depiction of coordination failure. Draupadi asks: if you've lost yourself, can you stake me? No elder answers. The question becomes core to legal philosophy: when does dharma itself become adharma through systemic application? Results in Kurukshetra war—18-day battle, near-total annihilation.

The Iliad

Homer, ~8th century BCE

Agamemnon takes Achilles' war prize Briseis to save face. Achilles withdraws from battle. Greeks suffer catastrophic losses. Patroclus dies trying to compensate. Achilles returns in grief-fueled rage.

Neither wants this outcome

But status competition prevents compromise

Thucydides later codified this as the "Melian dialogue" pattern—power without wisdom produces predictable tragedies. The whole Trojan War is coordination failure: war neither side wanted, triggered by coordination breakdown at every level.

Why Epic Scale Matters

  • Cannot be resolved by individual heroism
  • Multiple perspectives show interconnection
  • Scope reveals the system, not just individuals
  • Volume proves there's no escape
Epic length is structurally necessary for systemic narrative. Can't show coordination failure in single short story with single protagonist—need scope to show how locally rational choices interlock.

19th Century Realism

Balzac, Zola, Dickens

STACK: 19th Century Realism ───────────────── 1. Balzac's La Comédie Humaine 2. Zola's Rougon-Macquart 3. Dickens' social systems 4. The series format ───────────────── NAVIGATE: ↓ to dive in · → to skip to modern era

Balzac's La Comédie Humaine

90+ interconnected novels, 1829–1850

  • Market society creates systematic corruption
  • Individuals make locally rational choices
  • Collective result: moral degradation
  • Lost Illusions: journalism system where no individual can change it without destruction
Balzac mapping how emergence of market society creates coordination traps. Each novel shows different sector corrupted by market logic: journalism, family/money, revenge/resentment, etc.

Zola's Rougon-Macquart

20 novels, 1871–1893

  • Naturalist determinism: social conditions produce inevitable outcomes nobody wants
  • Germinal: miners know strike will fail, but what else can they do?
  • L'Assommoir: poverty creates drinking, drinking creates poverty

The system is the villain, not individuals

Zola's heredity/environment framework showing structural coordination failure. System produces outcomes independent of individual will or virtue.

Dickens' Social Systems

  • Bleak House: Jarndyce case consumes itself in legal fees
  • Little Dorrit: Circumlocution Office exists to prevent anything from being done
  • Hard Times: industrial capitalism grinds everyone

Institutions that serve themselves rather than stated purpose

Dickens showing how institutions become self-perpetuating even when dysfunctional. Each lawyer in Jarndyce vs Jarndyce acts rationally within system, but collectively they consume the entire estate in fees.

Why the Series Format?

Large interconnected novel series = literary form for depicting coordination failures

  1. Scope to show the system, not just individuals
  2. Multiple perspectives reveal the trap
  3. Volume proves there's no escape
  4. Seriality mirrors grinding repetition of the trap
Can't capture coordination failure in single novel with single protagonist. If it was just one book, you could imagine hero fixing it. Series format IS the argument: this is structural, systematic, inescapable.

Modern Era

Children's literature, prestige TV, literary fiction

Dr. Seuss: A Trilogy

  • The Lorax (1971)
    Tragedy of the commons
  • The Sneetches (1961)
    Status arms race
  • The Butter Battle Book (1984)
    Mutually assured destruction—no resolution, both sides poised to drop bomb
Remarkable that children's author produced clearest modern coordination-failure narratives. Butter Battle Book is particularly striking—ends without resolution because there IS no resolution to MAD logic.

The Wire (2002–2008)

David Simon

  • Five seasons examining institutional dysfunction
  • Police, unions, government, schools, media
  • Each dysfunctions in structurally similar ways
  • "Cynical about institutions" while humanistic toward individuals

Ends with system reasserting itself—everything as it started, different faces in same positions

Perhaps most successful systemic narrative in popular culture. Now taught at Harvard, York, Paris-Nanterre as sociology. Shows Baltimore as system where every institution trapped in perverse incentives, everyone makes locally rational choices that collectively fail, no individual villain.

Kafka, Heller, Kubrick

  • Kafka: gave English "Kafkaesque"—impersonal institutional dysfunction
  • Heller's Catch-22: gave English term for irresolvable systemic paradoxes
  • Kubrick's Dr. Strangelove: nuclear deterrence system that, once triggered, cannot be stopped
All three showing systems where no individual is malicious but collective apparatus produces catastrophe. Dr. Strangelove: combined will of every leader on earth cannot stop the system once activated.

The Pattern of Endings

13 of 20 most prominent modern coordination-failure narratives offer no solution or only individual exit

  • Catch-22: desertion
  • The Wire: system reasserts
  • Don't Look Up: extinction

The absence of satisfying solutions is not a bug—it's a feature that reveals something true

Coordination failures genuinely lack easy resolutions. Belling the Cat has no answer. Mahabharata's dice game has no rescue. The most honest coordination-failure stories end badly—their honesty makes them useful as diagnostic tools.

Conclusion

STACK: Conclusion ───────────────── 1. What effective stories do 2. Why absence of solutions matters 3. The meta coordination failure ───────────────── NAVIGATE: ↓ to dive in

What Effective Stories Do

They exploit rather than fight our agent-detection bias

  • Emperor's New Clothes: swindlers are characters
  • The Wire: system has human faces across 60 episodes
  • Meditations on Moloch: personifies system as demon

Populate the system with characters who are individually sympathetic and collectively trapped

Cannot tell systemic story by removing all agents. Must give the system human faces so our narrative equipment can engage.

Diagnosis, Not Prescription

The best coordination-failure stories install pattern recognition, not solutions

  • Belling the Cat: names the problem
  • Mahabharata: shows how systems trap even the wise
  • The Wire: reveals institutional logic

Their honesty about lack of easy answers is what makes them useful

Elinor Ostrom's real-world work on commons governance—showing communities CAN manage shared resources—has been far less culturally influential than Hardin's parable of doom. Institution-building is dramatically boring. The most honest coordination-failure stories end badly.

The Meta Coordination Failure

The scarcity of these stories may itself be a coordination failure

  • Each storyteller rationally defaults to villain narratives (more engaging, more marketable)
  • Collective result: culture poorly equipped to think about its most important problems
  • We have thousands of stories about evil kings
  • Zero mainstream narratives about credential inflation, institutional isomorphism, or why everyone checks email during meetings
The stories we need most are the stories hardest to tell. That difficulty is not coincidence—it's the coordination failure's final defense mechanism: invisible to the very cognitive tools we use to make things visible.

A Representational Gap

Our most common collective problems are coordination failures:

  • Climate change
  • Institutional corruption
  • Arms races
  • Credential inflation
  • Erosion of trust

Our storytelling equipment is designed for agent-centric narrative

We can tell story of villain poisoning river. We struggle to tell story of thousand rational actors collectively poisoning aquifer. This gap between our problems and our narrative tools may be one of the most consequential gaps in humanity's collective cognitive toolkit.
