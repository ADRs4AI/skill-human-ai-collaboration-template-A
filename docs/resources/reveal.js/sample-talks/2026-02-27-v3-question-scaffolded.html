the analytical lens for tonight

Two ways to use any intelligence

⚙ Cybernetic
"What output will satisfy the feedback loop?"
Outcome-oriented. You want something to look right, sound right, pass the gate.
💡 Epistemic
"How does this refine my understanding?"
Truth-oriented. You want to actually know something better.

Almost every controversy about AI comes down to this distinction. We'll use it all night.

This is THE frame. Return to it with every question.

one metaphor before we start

The Eager Intern

AI is like a really eager intern who never gets tired, knows a little about everything, loves to help brainstorm — and sometimes makes stuff up.

What do you do with an eager intern? You give them tasks. You check their work. You don't abdicate judgment.

deepfakes & truth

we've survived this before

deepfakes · historical parallel

Photography Was Also "Objective Truth"

When photography arrived, people believed: "the camera cannot lie."

Then: photomontage, darkroom manipulation, Stalin airbrushing people out of history. Then: digital editing, Photoshop.

We've learned — slowly — to be skeptical of photos. We can learn the same about AI-generated media. The adjustment is hard. It's not unprecedented.
Also: paintings were NEVER considered direct representations of reality. We extended documentary authority to photography specifically — and now have to partially take it back. This is a cultural adjustment, not a technical one. Culture can make these adjustments.

deepfakes · the real crisis

The Echo Bubble Problem

The deepfake threat isn't just fake videos. It's the collapse of shared epistemic ground.

  • People can't change their minds because they're in closed information loops
  • Information exchanges are no longer epistemic — they're tribal
Counterintuitive: AI can be part of the solution. AI can help you find the strongest counterargument to your position, explore evidence you haven't considered, slow down before forwarding. But only if used epistemically.
The trust question can't be answered by "trust more" or "trust less." It can only be answered by building the habit of asking better questions. AI can help with that — or make it worse. The distinction is the mode.

creativity

whose voice is it, anyway?

creativity · authorship

Ted Chiang's 10,000 Choices

Ted Chiang wrote Story of Your Life — the basis for the film Arrival.

He argues: a 10,000-word story represents ~10,000 specific word choices. Each choice is an act of authorship.

If you give AI a 100-word prompt and get 10,000 words back: whose story is it?
You've authored ~1% of it. The rest is pattern-matching on everything AI was trained on.
Photography parallel: when photography arrived, painters feared extinction. Reality: photography freed painting from documentary obligation → Impressionism, Cubism, Surrealism. Total artistic employment increased. What new forms will AI free us to create?

creativity · the historical comfort

Photography didn't kill painting

When photography arrived, portrait painters feared extinction.

Photography freed painting from its documentary obligation.

Result: Impressionism, Cubism, Surrealism, Abstract Expressionism — entire movements that couldn't have existed without photography forcing the question.

Total artistic employment increased after photography. The question: what new creative forms will AI force us to find?

creativity · two modes

How you use it is everything

Cybernetic use:
"Write me a novel"

No one's novel. Satisfies the output requirement but empties authorship.

Epistemic use:
"I'm stuck on chapter 3. What's the emotional truth I'm avoiding?"

You remain the author. AI is the writing partner who asks hard questions.

The creativity concern is real. The answer is about which mode we cultivate.

education & thinking

is it making us smarter or dumber?

education · the real concern

It's not about cheating

Students have always been able to cheat. The question is whether they learn.

The actual concern: using AI to bypass the productive struggle that is how thinking develops.

Productive struggle ✓ — wrestling with contradictions, formulating precise questions, defending your reasoning — builds intellectual capacity.

Needless friction ✗ — formatting citations, keyword searching — AI should eliminate this.

education · what schools actually do

The range of responses

Mostly failing responses:

  • Blanket bans (ignored immediately)
  • AI detection tools (unreliable, harmful false positives)
  • Return to handwritten exams (creates two-tier assessment)

More thoughtful responses:

  • Process-based assessment (show your work)
  • Oral examination and defense
  • AI-integrated assignments (use AI, then critique its output)
  • Teaching the cybernetic/epistemic distinction explicitly

education · the test that matters

The metacognition question

A student who uses AI to generate a draft they then revise, debate, and genuinely engage with has used it epistemically. A student who submits the AI's first draft has used it cybernetically.

The difference isn't the tool. It's the relationship to your own thinking.

After using AI for something, ask: "Did that make me think harder — or help me avoid thinking?"

the eager intern

and why not all interns are the same

the eager intern · the landscape

The main tools — what's actually different

  • ChatGPT (OpenAI): The most widely known. Very capable. Optimized heavily for user satisfaction — which can mean telling people what they want to hear.
  • Claude (Anthropic): Designed with more emphasis on honesty and "refusing to please at the expense of truth." Stronger for long documents and nuanced analysis.
  • Gemini (Google): Best integration with Google's search and document ecosystem.
The honest answer: They're all variations on the same underlying approach. The differences matter for specific tasks, but less than the way you use any of them.
Judy's observation about "lost veracity with use over time" is interesting. This isn't usually a technical degradation. It's more often: (1) The novelty wears off and you notice errors you overlooked before (2) Your use cases shift to harder things as you get more confident (3) OpenAI actually HAS been making tradeoffs toward engagement over accuracy in some updates Point 3 is real and worth acknowledging.

the eager intern · using any of them well

The mode matters more than the tool

What they're all good at:

  • Drafting and editing text
  • Explaining complex things simply
  • Brainstorming options
  • Summarizing long documents
  • Being available 24/7, no judgment

Where they all fall short:

  • Facts requiring exactness (verify everything important)
  • Recent events (training cutoffs)
  • Long logical chains
  • Real-world judgment

Best use of any of them: sparring partner, not oracle. "Help me see what I'm missing" beats "give me the answer."

labor & automation

who gets displaced, who gets augmented?

labor · the on-ramp

The Entry-Level Problem

AI currently handles: boilerplate code, documentation, simple scripts, debugging common errors.

What it can't replace: system design, understanding user needs, debugging complex emergent behavior, making tradeoffs under uncertainty.

The real risk: junior programmers learned by doing the boilerplate. If AI does that, how do they develop judgment? This is the entry-level crisis — and it's real.
For the software question specifically: 1 year → AI handles more boilerplate, speeds up coding significantly. 2 years → many junior-level tasks automated, but demand for software has always grown with capability. 5 years → very uncertain. Physical robots are a different problem from language models.

labor · the ATM lesson

The Bank Teller Story

ATMs arrived in the 1970s. Prediction: bank tellers would be eliminated.

What happened: ATMs cut tellers per branch from ~21 to ~13. But cheaper branches → banks opened MORE branches → total teller employment increased.

James Bessen's finding: Automation often increases demand for human work by making services cheaper and more accessible. The question is always: what happens to demand for the service?

labor · jevons paradox

The Efficiency Trap

As coal steam engines became more efficient, Britain used more coal — not less. Lower cost of coal use → more industries adopted steam → total consumption soared. — William Stanley Jevons, The Coal Question (1865)

Applied to software: as AI makes coding cheaper, more software gets written. The on-ramp problem is real — the "all jobs gone" prediction probably isn't. Narayanan's finding for lawyers: legal AI tools increased lawyer hours billed, not decreased them.

labor · the robot question

On Home Robots: A Separate Hard Problem

Language models (what ChatGPT, Claude are) excel at: text, code, reasoning about language.

Physical robots need: cameras, motors, real-time physics computation, manipulation of irregular objects, all in an uncontrolled environment.

Honest estimate: Some narrow tasks already exist (Roomba-style, some lawn mowers). A robot that can reliably "paint your house" in diverse conditions: further than any company will tell you. Physical world ≠ language world.
Geoffrey Hinton said in 2016 that radiologists would be replaced within 5 years. 2026: Radiology is one of the fastest-growing medical specialties. Even the best experts get these timelines wrong — badly. Humility about timelines is the only honest position.

power & control

who owns the future?

power · existential risk honestly

Taking it seriously without taking it literally

Do serious researchers think AI poses existential risks? Yes — some of them do.

Is there consensus? No. Deeply contested.

What is NOT contested: AI poses very real present risks — concentration of power, algorithmic bias, erosion of information ecosystems, labor displacement, privacy violations. These are happening now. They don't require superintelligence.
The honest position: I can't rule out long-term existential risk. Nobody can. What I can say: the companies making the loudest existential risk claims are often the same ones seeking massive investment and regulatory influence. Follow the money before following the fear.

power · the atomic comparison

AI vs. Atomic Weapons: A Real Comparison

Similarities:

  • Both require massive infrastructure to develop
  • Both concentrated in a few powerful actors initially
  • Both have obvious dual-use potential
  • Both outpaced governance frameworks

Key differences:

  • Atomic: immediate physical destruction, comprehensible mechanism
  • AI: no clear "detonation" — harm is diffuse and structural
  • Atomic: can be counted, verified, contained (treaties work)
  • AI: impossible to verify or "contain" in the same way
The comparison is useful for thinking about governance. The analogy breaks down when thinking about the mechanism of harm.

power · the controversies

xAI, Anthropic, and the Government

Both companies are navigating a new relationship with federal government — and not always straightforwardly.

The pattern (for any AI company):
Warn publicly about catastrophic AI risk → Become expert witnesses in the regulatory process → Shape regulations that benefit incumbents → Raise barriers to entry for competitors → Protect market position

This doesn't mean their concerns are fake. It means you should hold concern AND financial motive in mind simultaneously.

Be specific without being partisan. The xAI/Anthropic controversies span multiple administrations. The structural dynamic — regulatory capture by AI companies — is bipartisan. Offer to follow up after class on specific recent events (these change fast).

prediction ≠ understanding

being right for the wrong reason

prediction ≠ understanding · history

The Epicycles Problem

For over 1,000 years, Ptolemy's Earth-centered model made excellent predictions. It was also completely wrong.

AI systems can predict accurately while understanding nothing about why. Good benchmark scores ≠ good models of reality.
"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." — Goodhart's Law

With AI: this corruption happens instantly, at scale, automatically.

hallucinations

when "cannot compute" sounds like expertise

hallucinations · why

Why does it make things up?

  • It doesn't know it doesn't know
  • Trained to produce fluent, confident-sounding text
  • When it hits a gap: generates the most plausible-sounding continuation
Reframe: Hallucinations are diagnostic. Where a model hallucinates tells you something interesting about what it doesn't know.

Real case: a lawyer submitted AI-generated case citations that didn't exist. The AI invented plausible-sounding references. Fluency ≠ accuracy.

stochastic parrots

a catchy critique — and its complications

stochastic parrots · the paper

The Critique That Got Someone Fired

2021: Bender, Gebru et al. "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots." LLMs generate plausible text by statistical pattern-matching, without grounding in meaning.

Google fired Timnit Gebru after she refused to retract a related paper. The firing became its own major story.

Ted Chiang's "The Great Silence" (2015): a parrot's perspective. Parrots are social, emotionally complex, genuinely communicative — not mindless mimics. The metaphor has edges.

the environment

data centers, water, carbon — and Jevons again

the environment · costs and efficiency

Real costs, real progress

  • Training: very energy-intensive. Inference: adds up at scale. Water cooling: significant.
  • Scaffolding metaphor: Like early railroads — operating at losses to prove value, betting efficiency follows adoption.
  • Distillation: Large teacher → small student. GPT-4-level at fraction of cost.
  • Sparse models (MoE), LoRA, ASICs: Etched's Sohu chip is hardwired for transformer inference — orders of magnitude more efficient than general-purpose GPUs.

Jevons Paradox: efficiency → more adoption → more total use. Not automatically bad if powered by renewables.
